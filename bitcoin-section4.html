How It Works 04 / 09

Follow a transaction
from wallet to chain

No banks. No intermediaries. No permission required. A Bitcoin transaction is a cryptographically signed instruction that travels peer-to-peer across thousands of nodes worldwide, gets validated, mined into a block, and permanently recorded. Here is exactly how that happens — step by step.

Stage 01 — Wallet

UTXOs &
Private Keys

Your wallet doesn't hold bitcoin the way a bank holds dollars. It holds private keys — cryptographic secrets that prove you control certain unspent transaction outputs (UTXOs).

Think of UTXOs like physical coins of specific denominations. To send 0.3 BTC, your wallet selects UTXOs whose total value covers the amount — say, one UTXO of 0.5 BTC. It spends that UTXO entirely, sends 0.3 BTC to the recipient, and returns 0.197 BTC to yourself as change (the remainder is the miner fee).

Your private key signs the transaction with ECDSA (or Schnorr post-Taproot), producing a signature that proves ownership without revealing the key itself.

Your bitcoin never "move" — what changes is which UTXO is spendable by which key. The ledger records outputs, not balances.

Stage 02 — Broadcast

The Network
Receives It

The signed transaction is broadcast to the Bitcoin network — a peer-to-peer mesh of approximately 15,000+ reachable nodes worldwide. Your wallet sends it to a handful of nodes it's connected to. Each node validates it and relays it onwards. Within seconds, the transaction has propagated globally.

Every node performs the same checks independently: is the signature valid? Are the UTXOs unspent? Does the fee meet the minimum relay threshold? No single node is in charge. Consensus is the result of thousands of independent validators reaching the same conclusion.

If any check fails — forged signature, already-spent UTXO, zero fee — every honest node silently drops the transaction. It never reaches the mempool. No error message. No appeal.

There is no central server. "The network" is every node simultaneously. It cannot be taken down by targeting one point.

Stage 03 — Mempool

Waiting
Room

The mempool (memory pool) is a holding area of validated but unconfirmed transactions. Every node maintains its own mempool. Your transaction waits here until a miner includes it in a block.

Miners are economically rational. They sort the mempool by fee rate (satoshis per virtual byte) and pick the highest-paying transactions first. If the mempool is congested, low-fee transactions can wait hours — or days. If you're in a hurry, you pay more. If you can wait, you pay less. The fee market is a pure auction.

One sat = 0.00000001 BTC — the smallest unit. A typical transaction is ~226 vbytes. At 10 sat/vbyte, that's 2,260 sats ≈ a few dollars. At 100 sat/vbyte, it's 10× that.

Your tx: 13.3 sat/vbyte — competitive but not urgent. Estimated wait: next 1–3 blocks (~10–30 min).

Stage 04 — Mining

Proof
of Work

A miner assembles a candidate block — selecting transactions from the mempool, adding a coinbase transaction (their block reward + fees), and constructing a block header.

They then run SHA-256 hashing at enormous speed, incrementing a nonce (a 32-bit number) billions of times per second, searching for a hash output that starts with enough leading zeros to satisfy the current difficulty target. This cannot be faked or shortcut. It requires real energy. That expenditure is the proof.

The difficulty adjusts every 2,016 blocks (~2 weeks) to keep average block time at 10 minutes, regardless of how much hashrate joins or leaves the network. It is the most reliable clock in existence.

Global hashrate: ~700 EH/s (700 × 10¹⁸ hashes/sec). Odds of any single hash winning: 1 in ~10²²

Stage 05 — Confirmed

Immutable.
Forever.

Your transaction is now in a block. That block is linked cryptographically to every block before it — the hash of the previous block is embedded in each new block header. The chain is the ledger.

Each subsequent block mined on top adds another confirmation. After 6 confirmations (~1 hour), your transaction is considered irreversible in practice. To reverse it, an attacker would need to redo all that proof-of-work — and outpace the honest network doing it. At Bitcoin's hashrate, that is computationally impossible.

The recipient's wallet now shows a new UTXO they control. Your UTXO was consumed. The change UTXO is spendable by you. No bank processed this. No company approved it. No government can reverse it.

This transaction is now part of a ledger that will exist as long as a single node runs anywhere on earth.
