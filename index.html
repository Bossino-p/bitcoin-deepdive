The system
breaks

Lehman Brothers collapses on September 15, 2008. Within weeks, governments across the world begin the largest coordinated money-printing exercise in modern history. Trillions conjured from nothing to rescue institutions that had gambled with everyone else's savings — and lost.

The people who caused the crisis were made whole. The people who had nothing to do with it were handed the bill. This was not a bug. It was the system working exactly as designed: gains are privatised, losses are socialised, and the mechanism that makes it possible is the monopoly on money creation.

Somewhere, someone was already writing the alternative.

Oct 31 2008

Nine pages
change everything

On Halloween 2008, a message appears on a cryptography mailing list from someone calling themselves Satoshi Nakamoto. No institution behind the name. No credentials offered. Just a link and a subject line: "Bitcoin: A Peer-to-Peer Electronic Cash System."

Nine pages. The whitepaper solves a problem that had stumped cryptographers for decades — how do you prevent someone from spending the same digital money twice, without a trusted third party in the middle? The answer is the blockchain: a public ledger, maintained by everyone, owned by no one, tampered with by no one.

Satoshi's identity remains unknown to this day. It doesn't matter. The code was released. Bitcoin belongs to no one — which means it belongs to everyone.

Nine days after the genesis block, Satoshi sends 10 bitcoin to Hal Finney — a cryptographer, a cypherpunk, one of the few people paying attention. Transaction 4a5e1e. The first peer-to-peer transfer of value with no bank, no intermediary, no permission required from anyone.

For the next two years, Bitcoin has no price. It is an experiment. A curiosity. A proof that it could be done. The people involved are not investors — they are believers in a protocol.

That is what Bitcoin still is, under everything else that has been layered on top of it.

2009– 2010

Before
the price

May 22, 2010: a programmer named Laszlo Hanyecz pays 10,000 bitcoin for two pizzas. The first real-world commercial transaction. At the time, a reasonable trade. Today, those 10,000 BTC are worth hundreds of millions of dollars — and Bitcoin Pizza Day is celebrated every year not as a mistake, but as a milestone. Bitcoin had a price. The experiment was real.

Satoshi disappears from public communication in late 2010, handing the project to the developer community. No farewell. No exit interview. Just gone — leaving behind an open protocol, a fixed supply, and a running network.

Everything that follows is the story of what happens when you release genuinely neutral money into a world that has never had it.

The genesis block has been in continuous operation since January 3, 2009.
Over 900,000 blocks mined. Zero downtime. Zero bailouts. Zero exceptions.

Bitcoin's most dangerous moment — not from outside, but from within. A coalition of large miners, companies, and developers push to increase the block size, arguing it's needed for scale. How It Works 04 / 09

Follow a transaction
from wallet to chain

No banks. No intermediaries. No permission required. A Bitcoin transaction is a cryptographically signed instruction that travels peer-to-peer across thousands of nodes worldwide, gets validated, mined into a block, and permanently recorded. Here is exactly how that happens — step by step.

› › › ›
Stage 01 — Wallet

UTXOs &
Private Keys

Your wallet doesn't hold bitcoin the way a bank holds dollars. It holds private keys — cryptographic secrets that prove you control certain unspent transaction outputs (UTXOs).

Think of UTXOs like physical coins of specific denominations. To send 0.3 BTC, your wallet selects UTXOs whose total value covers the amount — say, one UTXO of 0.5 BTC. It spends that UTXO entirely, sends 0.3 BTC to the recipient, and returns 0.197 BTC to yourself as change (the remainder is the miner fee).

Your private key signs the transaction with ECDSA (or Schnorr post-Taproot), producing a signature that proves ownership without revealing the key itself.

Your bitcoin never "move" — what changes is which UTXO is spendable by which key. The ledger records outputs, not balances.

Stage 02 — Broadcast

The Network
Receives It

The signed transaction is broadcast to the Bitcoin network — a peer-to-peer mesh of approximately 15,000+ reachable nodes worldwide. Your wallet sends it to a handful of nodes it's connected to. Each node validates it and relays it onwards. Within seconds, the transaction has propagated globally.

Every node performs the same checks independently: is the signature valid? Are the UTXOs unspent? Does the fee meet the minimum relay threshold? No single node is in charge. Consensus is the result of thousands of independent validators reaching the same conclusion.

If any check fails — forged signature, already-spent UTXO, zero fee — every honest node silently drops the transaction. It never reaches the mempool. No error message. No appeal.

There is no central server. "The network" is every node simultaneously. It cannot be taken down by targeting one point.

Stage 03 — Mempool

Waiting
Room

The mempool (memory pool) is a holding area of validated but unconfirmed transactions. Every node maintains its own mempool. Your transaction waits here until a miner includes it in a block.

Miners are economically rational. They sort the mempool by fee rate (satoshis per virtual byte) and pick the highest-paying transactions first. If the mempool is congested, low-fee transactions can wait hours — or days. If you're in a hurry, you pay more. If you can wait, you pay less. The fee market is a pure auction.

One sat = 0.00000001 BTC — the smallest unit. A typical transaction is ~226 vbytes. At 10 sat/vbyte, that's 2,260 sats ≈ a few dollars. At 100 sat/vbyte, it's 10× that.

Your tx: 13.3 sat/vbyte — competitive but not urgent. Estimated wait: next 1–3 blocks (~10–30 min).

Stage 04 — Mining

Proof
of Work

A miner assembles a candidate block — selecting transactions from the mempool, adding a coinbase transaction (their block reward + fees), and constructing a block header.

They then run SHA-256 hashing at enormous speed, incrementing a nonce (a 32-bit number) billions of times per second, searching for a hash output that starts with enough leading zeros to satisfy the current difficulty target. This cannot be faked or shortcut. It requires real energy. That expenditure is the proof.

The difficulty adjusts every 2,016 blocks (~2 weeks) to keep average block time at 10 minutes, regardless of how much hashrate joins or leaves the network. It is the most reliable clock in existence.

Global hashrate: ~700 EH/s (700 × 10¹⁸ hashes/sec). Odds of any single hash winning: 1 in ~10²²

Stage 05 — Confirmed

Immutable.
Forever.

Your transaction is now in a block. That block is linked cryptographically to every block before it — the hash of the previous block is embedded in each new block header. The chain is the ledger.

Each subsequent block mined on top adds another confirmation. After 6 confirmations (~1 hour), your transaction is considered irreversible in practice. To reverse it, an attacker would need to redo all that proof-of-work — and outpace the honest network doing it. At Bitcoin's hashrate, that is computationally impossible.

The recipient's wallet now shows a new UTXO they control. Your UTXO was consumed. The change UTXO is spendable by you. No bank processed this. No company approved it. No government can reverse it.

This transaction is now part of a ledger that will exist as long as a single node runs anywhere on earth.

// 005 — Protocol

Technical
Deep Dives

SegWit (BIP141) separated signature data from transaction data, solving transaction malleability and increasing effective block capacity without raising the hard block size limit. Blocks can hold up to ~4 MB under the new weight unit system.

The malleability fix was the key prerequisite for the Lightning Network. Without it, off-chain payment channels cannot be built safely.

Activation was contentious. A miner-led effort to hard-fork (SegWit2x) was defeated by economic nodes running Bitcoin Core. It proved miners do not control the protocol. Users do.

Taproot (BIPs 340/341/342) introduced Schnorr signatures, MAST (Merkelized Abstract Syntax Trees), and Tapscript — improving privacy, efficiency, and smart contract expressiveness simultaneously.

Schnorr signatures are linear, enabling key aggregation: a multisig transaction looks identical on-chain to a single-sig. Complex spending conditions stay hidden until — and only if — they are exercised.

Activated at block 709,632 on November 14, 2021, via the Speedy Trial signaling mechanism, with near-unanimous miner support.

Lightning is a Layer 2 payment channel network. Two parties lock funds in a 2-of-2 multisig on-chain, then transact off-chain via signed-but-unbroadcast transactions. Only the open and close touch the base layer.

Payments route across channels using HTLCs — hash time-locked contracts — enabling trustless multi-hop payments without counterparty trust. Misbehavior triggers a penalty transaction; the cheater loses the full channel balance.

Near-instant, sub-satoshi payments at negligible fees. The throughput the base layer was never designed for.

// 006 — Risk

Risk
Matrix

Quantum Computing

Low / Long

A cryptographically-relevant quantum computer could break elliptic curve keys. Timeline: decades. Bitcoin can migrate via soft fork to post-quantum signatures well before it becomes a live threat.

51% Attack

Low

Controlling >50% of hashrate allows double-spending recent transactions — not theft, not new issuance. At current hashrate, the energy and hardware cost makes this economically irrational.

Fee Cliff

Medium

Post-2140, miners earn fees only. If block space demand collapses, miner revenue drops and security degrades. Layer 2 must generate enough base-layer fee demand to keep the chain secure.

State Attacks

Medium

Governments can ban on-ramps, pressure miners, seize hardware. They cannot invalidate the network. China banned mining in 2021. Hashrate recovered to new all-time highs within months.

Protocol Bug

Low

A critical consensus bug could allow inflation. The 2010 value overflow CVE was patched in hours. Fifteen-plus years of adversarial review make undiscovered critical bugs increasingly unlikely.

AI-Accelerated Attacks

Low / Unknown

AI could accelerate cryptanalysis or social engineering against key holders. SHA-256 and secp256k1 hardness is not meaningfully threatened by current AI capabilities. A watch item, not a present threat.

// 007 — Meaning

What Bitcoin
Represents

Every monetary system in history has eventually been debased. Gold was replaced by paper. Paper became digital promises. Digital promises became quantitative easing. Each step increased the distance between money and the work it claims to represent.

Bitcoin re-anchors money to energy, time, and math. It cannot be printed. It cannot be seized without the private key. It cannot be stopped by a government, a bank, or a board of directors. It is a protocol, not a company. A network, not an asset class.

“If you don’t believe me or don’t get it, I don’t have time to try to convince you, sorry.” — Satoshi Nakamoto, 2010

Whether Bitcoin becomes the world's reserve monetary network, or remains a sovereignty tool for individuals, or something else entirely — it has already proven something irreversible: a credibly neutral monetary system, enforced by physics rather than trust, is possible. That idea cannot be uninvented.

Not your keys. Not your coins.
