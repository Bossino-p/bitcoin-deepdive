The system
breaks

Lehman Brothers collapses on September 15, 2008. Within weeks, governments across the world begin the largest coordinated money-printing exercise in modern history. Trillions conjured from nothing to rescue institutions that had gambled with everyone else's savings — and lost.

The people who caused the crisis were made whole. The people who had nothing to do with it were handed the bill. This was not a bug. It was the system working exactly as designed: gains are privatised, losses are socialised, and the mechanism that makes it possible is the monopoly on money creation.

Somewhere, someone was already writing the alternative.

Oct 31 2008

Nine pages
change everything

On Halloween 2008, a message appears on a cryptography mailing list from someone calling themselves Satoshi Nakamoto. No institution behind the name. No credentials offered. Just a link and a subject line: "Bitcoin: A Peer-to-Peer Electronic Cash System."

Nine pages. The whitepaper solves a problem that had stumped cryptographers for decades — how do you prevent someone from spending the same digital money twice, without a trusted third party in the middle? The answer is the blockchain: a public ledger, maintained by everyone, owned by no one, tampered with by no one.

Satoshi's identity remains unknown to this day. It doesn't matter. The code was released. Bitcoin belongs to no one — which means it belongs to everyone.

How It Works 04 / 10

Follow a transaction
from wallet to chain

No banks. No intermediaries. No permission required. A Bitcoin transaction is a cryptographically signed instruction that travels peer-to-peer across thousands of nodes worldwide, gets validated, mined into a block, and permanently recorded. Here is exactly how that happens — step by step.

› › › ›
Stage 01 — Wallet

UTXOs &
Private Keys

Your wallet doesn't hold bitcoin the way a bank holds dollars. It holds private keys — cryptographic secrets that prove you control certain unspent transaction outputs (UTXOs).

Think of UTXOs like physical coins of specific denominations. To send 0.3 BTC, your wallet selects UTXOs whose total value covers the amount — say, one UTXO of 0.5 BTC. It spends that UTXO entirely, sends 0.3 BTC to the recipient, and returns 0.197 BTC to yourself as change (the remainder is the miner fee).

Your private key signs the transaction with ECDSA (or Schnorr post-Taproot), producing a signature that proves ownership without revealing the key itself.

Your bitcoin never "move" — what changes is which UTXO is spendable by which key. The ledger records outputs, not balances.

Stage 02 — Broadcast

The Network
Receives It

The signed transaction is broadcast to the Bitcoin network — a peer-to-peer mesh of approximately 15,000+ reachable nodes worldwide. Your wallet sends it to a handful of nodes it's connected to. Each node validates it and relays it onwards. Within seconds, the transaction has propagated globally.

Every node performs the same checks independently: is the signature valid? Are the UTXOs unspent? Does the fee meet the minimum relay threshold? No single node is in charge. Consensus is the result of thousands of independent validators reaching the same conclusion.

If any check fails — forged signature, already-spent UTXO, zero fee — every honest node silently drops the transaction. It never reaches the mempool. No error message. No appeal.

There is no central server. "The network" is every node simultaneously. It cannot be taken down by targeting one point.

Stage 03 — Mempool

Waiting
Room

The mempool (memory pool) is a holding area of validated but unconfirmed transactions. Every node maintains its own mempool. Your transaction waits here until a miner includes it in a block.

Miners are economically rational. They sort the mempool by fee rate (satoshis per virtual byte) and pick the highest-paying transactions first. If the mempool is congested, low-fee transactions can wait hours — or days. If you're in a hurry, you pay more. If you can wait, you pay less. The fee market is a pure auction.

One sat = 0.00000001 BTC — the smallest unit. A typical transaction is ~226 vbytes. At 10 sat/vbyte, that's 2,260 sats ≈ a few dollars. At 100 sat/vbyte, it's 10× that.

Your tx: 13.3 sat/vbyte — competitive but not urgent. Estimated wait: next 1–3 blocks (~10–30 min).

Stage 04 — Mining

Proof
of Work

A miner assembles a candidate block — selecting transactions from the mempool, adding a coinbase transaction (their block reward + fees), and constructing a block header.

They then run SHA-256 hashing at enormous speed, incrementing a nonce (a 32-bit number) billions of times per second, searching for a hash output that starts with enough leading zeros to satisfy the current difficulty target. This cannot be faked or shortcut. It requires real energy. That expenditure is the proof.

The difficulty adjusts every 2,016 blocks (~2 weeks) to keep average block time at 10 minutes, regardless of how much hashrate joins or leaves the network. It is the most reliable clock in existence.

Global hashrate: ~700 EH/s (700 × 10¹⁸ hashes/sec). Odds of any single hash winning: 1 in ~10²²

Stage 05 — Confirmed

Immutable.
Forever.

Your transaction is now in a block. That block is linked cryptographically to every block before it — the hash of the previous block is embedded in each new block header. The chain is the ledger.

Each subsequent block mined on top adds another confirmation. After 6 confirmations (~1 hour), your transaction is considered irreversible in practice. To reverse it, an attacker would need to redo all that proof-of-work — and outpace the honest network doing it. At Bitcoin's hashrate, that is computationally impossible.

The recipient's wallet now shows a new UTXO they control. Your UTXO was consumed. The change UTXO is spendable by you. No bank processed this. No company approved it. No government can reverse it.

This transaction is now part of a ledger that will exist as long as a single node runs anywhere on earth.

Truth 01

Predictable Issuance

Every bitcoin that will ever exist follows a schedule written in 2008. On any given day you can calculate exactly how many bitcoin exist, how many will be mined tomorrow, and the precise block at which the last satoshi will be created. No central bank in history has offered this.

Truth 02

Asymptotic Scarcity

The supply curve approaches 21 million but never reaches it — the halving means each epoch issues half the previous. This is not a marketing claim. It is the mathematical property of a geometric series. 94% of all bitcoin will have been mined by 2024. The remaining 6% takes over 100 years.

Truth 03

The Fee Transition

As the block subsidy decays toward zero, miner revenue must come increasingly from transaction fees. This is Bitcoin's one open economic question — whether fee markets will provide sufficient security budget at scale. It is not a flaw. It is the honest design tension.

Every fiat currency ever created has trended toward zero purchasing power. Bitcoin is the first monetary asset with a mathematically enforced ceiling. Whether that makes it sound money or a speculative asset depends entirely on whether you trust the code or the committee.

Upgrades & Terms 06 / 10

The language
of Bitcoin

Bitcoin's base protocol is intentionally conservative — it changes rarely and only when the change is overwhelmingly adopted by the network. Each upgrade below earned its place through years of review, debate, and consensus. The terms below are the vocabulary every bitcoiner eventually learns.

Protocol upgrades

Segregated Witness separated the signature data (witness) from the transaction data and moved it to a separate part of the block. This solved a long-standing bug called transaction malleability — where a third party could alter a transaction's ID without invalidating it, which had blocked Lightning Network development.

SegWit also introduced virtual bytes (vbytes) as a weight measurement, giving witness data a 75% discount. This effectively increased block capacity from 1MB to ~1.7MB in practice — without changing the 1MB base block size limit that was at the heart of the block size wars.

Why it matters SegWit was the prerequisite for Lightning Network. Without it, payment channels couldn't be built safely. It also made transactions cheaper and faster — and it did it as a soft fork, meaning old nodes still validated new blocks.

Taproot bundled three improvements: Schnorr signatures, MAST (Merkelised Abstract Syntax Trees), and Tapscript. Together they make complex transactions — multi-signature wallets, Lightning channel opens, smart contracts — look identical to simple single-signature transactions on the blockchain.

This has two profound effects. First, privacy: a 2-of-3 multisig spend is now indistinguishable from a regular payment to any outside observer. Second, efficiency: only the executed spending condition needs to be revealed — all unused conditions remain hidden, saving block space.

Why it matters Taproot makes Bitcoin smarter without making it complex. It unlocks better multi-sig, more private Lightning channels, and lays the groundwork for more sophisticated smart contracts — all while keeping the base layer simple and auditable.

Bitcoin originally used ECDSA (Elliptic Curve Digital Signature Algorithm) to sign transactions. Taproot replaced this with Schnorr signatures — a mathematically simpler scheme with a crucial additional property: linearity.

Linearity means multiple signatures can be aggregated into one. In a 3-of-5 multisig wallet, instead of publishing three separate signatures (each taking block space), you publish a single aggregated signature that's the same size as a single-sig transaction. This is called MuSig.

Schnorr also has a simpler security proof than ECDSA, reducing cryptographic attack surface.

Why it matters Signature aggregation means multi-sig wallets are cheaper, faster, and indistinguishable from single-sig. It's a compounding win: more privacy + lower fees + quantum-resistance groundwork.

The base Bitcoin layer processes ~7 transactions per second. Visa does ~24,000. Lightning solves this by moving most transactions off-chain into payment channels.

Two parties open a channel by locking bitcoin in a multisig address on-chain. They can then send unlimited transactions between each other instantly, for near-zero fees, without touching the blockchain. When they're done, they settle the final balance on-chain with a single closing transaction.

Channels form a network — you don't need a direct channel with everyone. Payments route through intermediate nodes, like internet packets routing through servers, with each hop taking a tiny fee.

Why it matters Lightning makes Bitcoin usable for everyday payments — coffee, remittances, micropayments. It settles in milliseconds. It doesn't require trust in a third party. El Salvador's Chivo wallet runs on Lightning.

Ordinals assign a serial number to every satoshi based on the order it was mined. This lets individual sats be tracked and treated as unique. Using Taproot's expanded witness data capacity, arbitrary data — images, text, code — can be inscribed directly onto individual satoshis and stored permanently on the Bitcoin blockchain.

This created Bitcoin's first NFT-like ecosystem and sparked fierce debate. Critics argue inscriptions waste block space, drive up fees for regular users, and pollute the monetary network with jpegs. Supporters argue any legal use of block space is valid — the fee market decides, not a committee.

Why it matters Ordinals revealed that Bitcoin's base layer is more expressive than most assumed — and intensified the fee market debate. Whether you think it's noise or signal, it proved Bitcoin can't be stopped from being used in ways its community disagrees with. That cuts both ways.

Foundational concepts

Hash rate is the total number of SHA-256 hash computations the Bitcoin network performs every second. It is the aggregate of every ASIC miner running worldwide. Higher hash rate = more computational work required to attack the network.

A 51% attacker would need to match the honest network's hash rate — currently around ~700 EH/s (700 × 10¹⁸ hashes per second). At current hardware efficiency, that would require billions in ASICs and electricity, consumed faster than you could recoup it.

When China banned mining in 2021 and ~65% of hash rate went offline, the network kept producing blocks. The difficulty adjustment recalibrated within two weeks. Hash rate fully recovered within six months.

Why it matters Hash rate is the most honest measure of Bitcoin's security. It can't be faked, inflated, or politicked. It represents real energy expenditure by real economic actors who believe the network is worth protecting.

Bitcoin doesn't store balances. It stores a set of unspent transaction outputs — discrete chunks of bitcoin locked to specific addresses. Your "balance" is just the sum of UTXOs your keys control.

Think of UTXOs like physical banknotes of irregular denomination. If you have a 0.5 BTC UTXO and want to pay 0.2 BTC, you spend the entire UTXO as input and create two new outputs: 0.2 BTC to the recipient and ~0.297 BTC back to yourself as change (minus the miner fee). The original UTXO is destroyed. Two new UTXOs are born.

Why it matters The UTXO model makes Bitcoin's ledger stateless and parallelisable — each UTXO can be verified independently. It also makes double-spend prevention explicit: a UTXO is either unspent or it isn't. There's no ambiguous in-between.

1 Bitcoin = 100,000,000 satoshis (sats). Named after Satoshi Nakamoto, a sat is the smallest indivisible unit of bitcoin — 0.00000001 BTC. You don't need to buy a whole bitcoin. At $60,000 per BTC, one dollar buys roughly 1,667 sats.

The "stack sats" culture among bitcoiners is deliberate: framing accumulation in sats rather than dollars reorients thinking away from fiat price and toward how much of the fixed supply you control. There are only 2.1 quadrillion sats ever. Every sat you hold is a fixed fraction of the total.

Lightning Network transactions are denominated in millisatoshis (msats) — 1/1000th of a sat — for routing fee precision. These never touch the base chain.

Why it matters Thinking in sats reframes Bitcoin's value proposition. You're not buying a fraction of an expensive coin. You're acquiring a measurable, permanent share of the only provably scarce digital asset. Stack accordingly.

A full node downloads the entire blockchain and independently validates every transaction and block against Bitcoin's rules. No trust required. Your node is your vote — it enforces which rules it accepts. This is why the block size wars ended the way they did: users running nodes rejected the large-block fork.

A private key is a 256-bit random number. From it, a public key is derived (elliptic curve math). From the public key, a Bitcoin address is derived (hashing). The private key signs transactions. "Not your keys, not your coins" — if an exchange holds your keys, they hold your bitcoin. Mt. Gox is the lesson.

Why it matters Running a node + self-custody is the full expression of Bitcoin's value proposition. Everything else is trust — and trust is what Bitcoin was built to eliminate. A hardware wallet and a Raspberry Pi node cost less than one month of bank fees.

Self-Custody 06b / 10

Not your keys, not your coins.

Everyone learns this phrase. Far fewer learn what comes next. Holding your own keys is the full expression of Bitcoin's value proposition. It also makes you the single point of failure. Here is what that actually requires.

More bitcoin has been lost to human error than to any protocol attack in Bitcoin's history. Lost seed phrases. Forgotten passphrases. Hardware wallets in flooded basements. Estates where no heir knew the bitcoin existed. Hard drives thrown away before the price mattered.

James Howells famously lost 8,000 BTC on a discarded hard drive in a Newport landfill. Stefan Thomas lost the password to 7,002 BTC on an IronKey with two attempts remaining. These are not edge cases. They are the dominant failure mode.

The Mt. Gox lesson is "not your keys, not your coins." The self-custody lesson is equally unforgiving: your keys, your full responsibility. There is no Ctrl+Z. There is no support ticket. There is no court order that reverses a lost seed.

Risks & how to handle them

Your seed phrase is the master key to every bitcoin in a wallet. Anyone who has it controls your funds. If it's destroyed, your funds are gone. It must be both secure and recoverable. These two requirements are in direct tension — which is why most people get it wrong.

Paper degrades. House fires are real. Floods happen. The standard for serious holdings is metal backup — seed phrases stamped or engraved into stainless steel plates that survive fire, water, and time. Never store your seed phrase digitally — not in a photo, not in a notes app, not in a password manager, not in the cloud. Ever.

The rule At minimum two physical copies in two geographically separate locations. Metal for anything above a few hundred dollars in value. No digital copies under any circumstances.

The term comes from a famous security joke: no amount of cryptographic protection matters if someone holds a wrench to your head and demands your seed phrase. Bitcoin's cryptography is unbreakable. Humans are not.

The mitigation is not heroism — it is plausible deniability. A passphrase wallet (the "25th word") creates a completely separate wallet at the same seed. You keep a decoy wallet with a small balance at the base seed. Under duress, you hand over the decoy. Your real holdings sit behind the passphrase — which the attacker doesn't know exists.

Operational security also matters: don't publicly announce your holdings. The person who can't be targeted can't be coerced.

The rule Never disclose holdings publicly. Use a passphrase wallet for significant amounts. Maintain a credible decoy wallet with a small balance at the base seed.

Multisig (multi-signature) requires M of N keys to sign a transaction. The most common setup is 2-of-3: three keys exist, any two can spend. One key can be lost without losing funds. One key can be stolen without the thief being able to move anything.

The keys live in different locations — one at home, one at a trusted location, one with a collaborative custody provider or in a safety deposit box. An attacker must compromise two separate locations simultaneously. A fire destroys one location — you still have two keys. Multisig is the gold standard for any significant holding.

Tools: Sparrow Wallet for desktop, Nunchuk for mobile-first multisig, Unchained or Casa for collaborative custody with one key held by a professional co-signer.

The rule Single-sig is acceptable for small amounts. For significant holdings — anything you'd be devastated to lose — 2-of-3 multisig is the minimum acceptable standard.

If you die tomorrow, can your family access your bitcoin? Do they know it exists? Do they know what a seed phrase is? Do they know where it's stored? For most self-custody holders, the answer to all four questions is no.

Bitcoin inheritance is a unique problem. Unlike a bank account, there is no institution to contact, no probate process that unlocks funds, no customer service that can help. The seed phrase is the estate. If no one has it, the estate is gone.

The solution is a documented inheritance plan — instructions clear enough that a non-technical heir can follow them, secure enough that a thief finding them early can't act on them. These two requirements require careful design, not just a note in a drawer.

The rule Your heirs need to know bitcoin exists, where to find the seed, and how to use it. Document it. Review it every year. A lawyer familiar with digital assets should be involved for large estates.
Inheritance planning steps

Document existence

A trusted heir needs to know bitcoin exists and approximately how much. Not the seed — just that it's there.

Write access instructions

Step-by-step guide assuming zero technical knowledge. Where is the hardware wallet? What software is needed? What is a seed phrase?

Secure the instructions separately from the seed

Instructions alone are useless to a thief. Seed alone is inaccessible to a grieving family. Keep them separate with a clear trigger mechanism (e.g. sealed envelope with a lawyer).

Consider multisig as inheritance tool

A 2-of-3 where one key is held by a collaborative custody provider gives your heir a guided recovery path through that provider.

Review annually

Wallet addresses change. Hardware wallets get replaced. Amounts change. Your plan must stay current.

🔑

Being your own bank is not a metaphor. It carries the same weight of responsibility that running a bank carries — without the staff, the regulators, or the insurance. Bitcoin gives you unprecedented financial sovereignty. What you do with that responsibility is entirely yours. Set it up properly. Document it. Review it. The protocol will outlast all of us — make sure your holdings can too.

Protocol threats

A sufficiently powerful quantum computer running Shor's algorithm could derive a private key from a public key, breaking ECDSA — the signature scheme Bitcoin currently uses. Addresses that have never spent (public key unknown) are safe. Reused addresses and exposed public keys are the first exposure. Satoshi's early coins — public keys long visible on-chain — are theoretically at risk.

NIST finalised its first post-quantum cryptography standards in 2024. Bitcoin can hard fork to quantum-resistant signatures (SPHINCS+, Lattice-based schemes). The honest question is not if Bitcoin will need to upgrade — it's whether the upgrade happens before a quantum machine capable of attacking ECDSA exists.

Verdict Not if, but when Bitcoin upgrades. The threat is real but the timeline gives the network room. Watch IBM and Google's qubit error-correction progress — that is the actual clock.

Controlling 51% of hashrate lets an attacker double-spend and reorder recent transactions — but not steal funds, reverse old blocks, or create new bitcoin. At Bitcoin's current hashrate of ~700 EH/s, a sustained attack would require billions in specialised hardware and electricity. The attacker would be destroying the value of the asset they're attacking to profit from double-spends worth a fraction of the cost.

Smaller proof-of-work chains have been 51% attacked — Bitcoin Gold, Bitcoin SV, Ethereum Classic. Bitcoin itself has never been successfully attacked. The honest miner incentive overwhelmingly dominates at Bitcoin's scale.

Verdict This is a real concern for altcoins, not Bitcoin. The attack cost exceeds any conceivable profit. A nation-state could attempt it — but they'd be publicly spending billions to achieve a temporary disruption.

In 2010, CVE-2010-5139 allowed someone to exploit an integer overflow and create 184 billion bitcoin out of thin air. Satoshi patched it in hours. The chain was rolled back. Bitcoin was 18 months old.

Since then, Bitcoin Core has become one of the most scrutinised codebases on earth. Changes require extensive peer review and broad consensus. The Lightning Network introduces a significantly larger attack surface — payment channels involve more complex state and time-sensitive operations that create new classes of exploitable edge cases.

Verdict The base protocol is battle-hardened. The 2010 incident is the proof of response capacity, not fragility. Lightning is the more active attack surface today. The open-source review culture is the primary defence.

External threats

AI lowers the cost of finding obscure vulnerabilities — automated code auditing could surface protocol bugs faster than human review catches them. AI-optimised social engineering makes targeting key holders, node operators, and developers dramatically cheaper and more convincing.

The same tools defend: AI-assisted mempool anomaly detection, real-time exploit monitoring, and automated smart contract auditing. Bitcoin's intentional simplicity is a structural advantage — a minimal scripting language has far less attack surface than Ethereum's Turing-complete EVM. The near-term risk is primarily to custody and human operators, not the protocol.

Verdict Genuine emerging threat through the tooling it gives adversaries. Bitcoin's minimal base layer is a real structural defence. The greater risk today is AI-enhanced phishing targeting self-custody holders — not AI attacking the protocol.

China banned Bitcoin mining in May 2021, eliminating ~65% of global hashrate overnight. Within two weeks difficulty adjusted. Within six months hashrate fully recovered, now distributed across the US, Kazakhstan, and Canada. The stress test already ran. Bitcoin passed.

A global coordinated ban would require unprecedented geopolitical alignment between adversarial nations. As Bitcoin becomes a strategic reserve asset — with the US, El Salvador, and others holding it — banning becomes self-harm. The protocol runs over Tor, Blockstream Satellite, and mesh networks. You cannot ban mathematics.

Verdict The China ban is the proof-of-concept. Geopolitical fragmentation actually protects decentralisation. The more nations hold Bitcoin, the less incentive any nation has to destroy it.

When the block subsidy reaches zero (~2140), miners must survive entirely on transaction fees. If fees are insufficient, mining becomes unprofitable, hashrate drops, and security degrades. This is called the fee cliff — and it is the most intellectually honest long-term concern in Bitcoin. Serious bitcoiners debate it openly.

The bull case: by 2140, Bitcoin's market cap makes even thin fees generate enormous absolute revenue. Lightning may consolidate transactions, boosting base-layer fee density. The bear case: if block space demand doesn't grow sufficiently, the security budget becomes inadequate at scale. There is no resolved answer. This is the one thing worth genuine intellectual humility on.

Verdict Not a threat for generations — but the one structural question the network will eventually answer through fee market evolution. Any bitcoiner who dismisses it has stopped thinking. Any critic who leads with it hasn't done the timeline math.

The block size wars (2015–2017) were an attempt to co-opt Bitcoin's roadmap through miner and corporate pressure. The result: Bitcoin Cash forked off and Bitcoin Core's conservative, node-first governance was vindicated.

Changing Bitcoin's consensus rules requires near-universal node adoption — not majority votes, not corporate sign-off, not miner signalling alone. There is no CEO, no foundation, no Vitalik. The social consensus has proven extraordinarily resistant to co-option. The ossification that frustrates developers is the same property that protects against capture.

Verdict The block size wars already ran this attack. Bitcoin didn't bend. The slowness of change is a feature, not a bug — it means no single actor can unilaterally alter the rules that protect every holder.

The honest summary

Every threat above has been considered by people smarter than its critics.

Bitcoin is not without risk. It has never claimed to be. What it has done is survive every practical attack launched against it for 16 years — exchange collapses, nation-state bans, civil wars, market crashes, regulatory sieges. The network has never missed a block.

The one thing to watch

Quantum computing is the only threat with a hard deadline.

Every other risk on this page is either already survived, economically irrational to execute, or a distant theoretical. Quantum has a real timeline. The migration to post-quantum cryptography needs to happen before a sufficiently powerful machine exists. That window is open. It will not stay open forever.

What It Means 10 / 10

This is not about money. It never was.

Every monetary system in history has rested on a single assumption: that someone, somewhere, can be trusted with the power to create money. Kings. Governments. Central banks. The institution changes. The assumption never does.

Bitcoin removes the assumption. It replaces trust in institutions with trust in mathematics — a protocol that runs the same rules for everyone, that no government can inflate, no company can seize, and no committee can change without the consent of every participant. That is not a financial product. It is a political act.

"The root problem with conventional currency is all the trust that's required to make it work. The central bank must be trusted not to debase the currency, but the history of fiat currencies is full of breaches of that trust."

— Satoshi Nakamoto, P2P Foundation Forum, February 11 2009

Bitcoin is the first technology in history that lets a person opt out of the monetary system entirely — to hold value that cannot be inflated away, confiscated by decree, or frozen by a bank. Not because of a legal right. Because of a mathematical property.

It will not fix human nature. It will not end corruption or greed. What it does is remove one instrument of that corruption — the monopoly on money creation — and place it beyond the reach of any single actor. That has never been done before.

Whether Bitcoin succeeds in its fullest ambition or not, it has already proven something irreversible: that sound, decentralised, censorship-resistant money is technically possible. That proof cannot be uninvented. The idea is out.

01

Separation of Money & State

Just as the printing press separated knowledge from institutional control, Bitcoin separates money from political control. The consequences will take generations to fully unfold.

Twelve words. Self-custody. No bank required, no account to freeze, no border to stop a transaction. Financial self-sovereignty available to anyone on earth with a phone and an internet connection.

There has never been a provably scarce asset before Bitcoin. Gold is scarce. But more can always be mined. 21 million is a mathematical ceiling, not a policy. It cannot be voted away.

Bitcoin doesn't care who you are, where you live, or what your government thinks of you. It validates transactions the same way for a billionaire in Manhattan and a farmer in Zimbabwe. The protocol has no prejudice.

An Exit

Not a revolution. Not a takeover. An exit. A door that anyone can walk through. The most peaceful form of dissent from a monetary system ever created — you simply stop participating.
